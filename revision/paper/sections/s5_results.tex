\section{Results}

\subsection{Why Session-Grouped Evaluation Is Required}
\label{sec:leak}
This analysis motivates the evaluation design; it quantifies a known pitfall~\cite{kapoor2023leakage,kaufman2012leakage} on these data and shows that a reconstruction of the earlier version's protocol yields similarly inflated scores, rather than introducing a new phenomenon. Table~\ref{tab:leak} evaluates the same 21 raw columns under three protocols. When every discomfort grade is oversampled to 5,243 samples before a 70/15/15 row split, an untuned gradient-boosting classifier reaches \LeakOldAcc\,\% accuracy, \LeakOldRecall\,\% recall, MCC \LeakOldMCC{} and ROC-AUC \LeakOldRocAuc; \LeakDupPos{} of the \LeakTestPos{} positive test rows (\LeakDupPct\,\%) have an exact duplicate in the training split, and every session contributes to both splits. Without oversampling, a row-level split still gives ROC-AUC \LeakRowRocAuc{} (accuracy \LeakRowAcc\,\%), because adjacent seconds of the same session are nearly identical and carry the same label. With a session-grouped split, the same classifier falls to ROC-AUC \LeakGrpRocAuc{} (MCC \LeakGrpMCC). The earlier E-DDQN implementation behaves the same way: \OrigOldRecall\,\% recall and MCC \OrigOldMCC{} on the oversampled row-level partition, but MCC \OrigGrpMCC{} on a grouped partition. Near-perfect scores on these data therefore measure the recognition of sessions seen during training, not the detection of cybersickness in new sessions, and they are obtained without any reinforcement learning. All results below use the leakage-controlled, session-grouped protocol of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}; session-linkage groups reduce, but cannot exclude, leakage across participants (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Session-Grouped Comparison under the 5:1 Cost (RQ2)}
\label{sec:main}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports every model tuned, checkpointed and thresholded on validation groups only and scored once on the outer-test groups (all \nSamplesEval{} samples, \nSessionsEval{} sessions). Because the labels are close to step functions and these seconds come from only \nGroupsEval{} independent groups and \nEventSessions{} sessions with an onset, the per-second metrics mainly measure how well a model reproduces when, and in which sessions, discomfort begins. Two facts dominate. First, the detection problem is hard once sessions and linkage groups are not shared: among the primary models, the best ROC-AUC is \HGBCostRocAucMean{} (HGB) and \RFCostRocAucMean{} (random forest), where 0.5 corresponds to chance, and the best PR-AUC is \DQNCostPrAucMean{} (standard DQN), where chance equals the prevalence of 0.44. Second, under a 5:1 cost the cost-minimizing operating point of every model lies close to ``always alarm'' (cost \AlwaysCost): validation-selected thresholds yield high recall but false-positive rates of \DOneFPRMin--\DOneFPRMax\,\%. The random forest has the lowest cost, both as a mean over seeds (\RFCostCostMean$\pm$\RFCostCostSD) and as a seed ensemble (\PHEnsRFCost); these are point estimates, and the differences among the supervised models were not tested.

With its primary search draw, E-DDQN has the highest cost and the lowest ROC-AUC of the \nModels{} primary models (point estimates; tests in Section~\ref{sec:stats}; other draws in Section~\ref{sec:why}): recall \EDDQNCostRecallMean$\pm$\EDDQNCostRecallSD\,\%, specificity \EDDQNCostSpecMean$\pm$\EDDQNCostSpecSD\,\%, cost \EDDQNCostCostMean$\pm$\EDDQNCostCostSD{} (above the \AlwaysCost{} of always alarming as a point estimate; not significant, Section~\ref{sec:stats}), ROC-AUC \EDDQNCostRocAucMean$\pm$\EDDQNCostRocAucSD{} (seed ensemble \EDDQNCostRocAucEns, 95\,\% CI \EDDQNCIRocAucLo{} to \EDDQNCIRocAucHi, which includes chance) and MCC \EDDQNCostMCCMean; its seed ensemble is worse still (cost \PHEnsEDDQNCost, recall \PHEnsEDDQNRecall\,\%), and its seed-ensemble cost is higher than the random forest's in \PHFoldsEDDQNWorseRF{} of 5 outer folds, by \PHFoldDiffEDDQNRFMin{} to \PHFoldDiffEDDQNRFMax{}, i.e.\ only marginally in one fold (Supplementary Sec.~S3.4). The standard DQN reaches cost \DQNCostCostMean{} and ROC-AUC \DQNCostRocAucMean, and the contextual-bandit variant E-CB cost \ECBCostCostMean{} and ROC-AUC \ECBCostRocAucMean. E-DDQN therefore does not improve on the standard DQN in this controlled comparison; with two further search draws it did not reach a significantly lower cost either, although it ranked seconds better than the DQN in one of them (Section~\ref{sec:why}).

\subsection{Exposure-Time Baselines}
\label{sec:time}
The post-hoc baselines (Table~\ref{tab:main}, Fig.~\ref{fig:roc}) show what the primary models have learned. Time-LR, a logistic regression on elapsed time alone, reaches ROC-AUC \PHTimeLRRocAucMean{} and PR-AUC \PHTimeLRPrAucMean, the highest pooled seed-mean values in Table~\ref{tab:main}. Computed within each outer fold and averaged, which avoids pooling the scores of five fold-specific models, the random forest ranks slightly higher (\PHWfRFAuc{} vs.\ \PHWfTimeLRAuc), followed by HGB (\PHWfHGBAuc), and E-DDQN remains last (\PHWfEDDQNAuc; Supplementary Sec.~S5.7) with its primary search draw, though not with other draws (up to \SDEDDQNWfMax; Section~\ref{sec:why}). Time-LR's pooled ROC-AUC does not differ significantly from that of the random forest (difference \PHTimeLRvsRFAuc, 95\,\% CI \PHTimeLRvsRFAucLow{} to \PHTimeLRvsRFAucHigh; group bootstrap); the interval is wide, so equivalence is not established either. At the 5:1 operating point, the random forest has a lower cost than Time-LR (seed ensembles \PHEnsRFCost{} vs.\ \PHEnsTimeLRCost; difference \PHTimeLRvsRFCost, CI \PHTimeLRvsRFCostLow{} to \PHTimeLRvsRFCostHigh; group bootstrap $p=$\,\PHTimeLRvsRFCostP, exact sign-flip $p=$\,\PHSfTimeLRvsRFP), with fewer missed events and fewer false alarms in the seed ensemble (Table~\ref{tab:main}). With \nGroupsEval{} groups the bootstrap $p$-value is approximate and the exact sign-flip $p$-value lies just below 0.05, so the evidence that the additional inputs help the forest at the operating point is suggestive rather than conclusive. Adding the game indicator (Time+game HGB) gives cost \PHTimeHGBCostMean{} and ROC-AUC \PHTimeHGBRocAucMean, again not significantly different from the random forest (cost difference \PHTimeHGBvsRFCost, $p=$\,\PHTimeHGBvsRFCostP{}, sign-flip $p=$\,\PHSfTimeHGBvsRFP; ROC-AUC difference \PHTimeHGBvsRFAuc, $p=$\,\PHTimeHGBvsRFAucP). Both minimal baselines rank seconds better than the primary E-DDQN, Time-LR clearly and Time+game HGB less conclusively (seed-ensemble ROC-AUC differences \PHTimeLRvsEDDQNAuc, CI \PHTimeLRvsEDDQNAucLow{} to \PHTimeLRvsEDDQNAucHigh, group bootstrap $p$\,\PHTimeLRvsEDDQNAucP, and \PHTimeHGBvsEDDQNAuc, CI \PHTimeHGBvsEDDQNAucLow{} to \PHTimeHGBvsEDDQNAucHigh, $p=$\,\PHTimeHGBvsEDDQNAucP, which is approximate with \nGroupsEval{} groups). On these data, therefore, no model was shown to discriminate better than a population onset curve, in which cybersickness becomes more likely the longer a session lasts, as long documented for exposure duration~\cite{kennedy2000duration}; the wide intervals leave open modest gains that a sample of \nGroupsEval{} groups cannot detect.

\subsection{Operating Points, Cost Ratios and the Asymmetric Reward}
\label{sec:op}
Table~\ref{tab:ops} contrasts three operating rules. With the untuned cut-off $\hat p\ge 1/6$ (rule D0)---for the asymmetric agents the greedy policy $\arg\max_a Q(s,a)$, which because of class-balanced replay corresponds for E-DDQN approximately to a cut-off of \SSEDDQNCutMin--\SSEDDQNCutMax{} on $P(y=1\mid s)$ rather than to the 5:1 Bayes cut-off of $1/6$ (Section~\ref{sec:formulation})---E-DDQN obtains recall \EDDQNBayesRecallMean\,\% at specificity \EDDQNBayesSpecMean\,\% (cost \EDDQNBayesCostMean), whereas the random forest obtains recall \RFBayesRecallMean\,\% at specificity \RFBayesSpecMean\,\% (cost \RFBayesCostMean). When every model is held to a validation recall of at least 0.95, the random forest again has the lowest cost (\RFRecCostMean; E-DDQN \EDDQNRecCostMean). At validation-matched recall targets (Table~\ref{tab:matched}; descriptive, no test), the thresholds transfer worst for E-DDQN: at both recall targets shown, and also at the targets 0.80 and 0.99 (released results), it attains lower test recall than every supervised model, and at the 0.95 target it also has a higher false-positive rate than all of them. Reading the ROC curves at fixed recall (descriptive only), the false-positive rate at 95\,\% recall is \EDDQNRocFPRatNineFive\,\% for E-DDQN and \RFRocFPRatNineFive\,\% for the random forest. Because the reward encodes the same 5:1 operating point as a cost-weighted classifier (Eq.~\eqref{eq:margin}), the asymmetric reward provided, on these data, no advantage that cost-weighted training plus a validation-selected threshold did not already provide.

The cost curves of Fig.~\ref{fig:costcurves} show how much the choice of the 5:1 ratio matters. At ratios of 1.5--2, where the cost-optimal policy is not close to a trivial one, the relative costs of all models except E-DDQN lie between \PHCcLowRMin{} and \PHCcLowRMax{} (random forest: \PHCcRFTwo{} at $R=2$). As $R$ grows, the Bayes cut-off $1/(1+R)$ falls far below the prevalence, always alarming becomes nearly optimal, and the room for improving on it shrinks: at $R=5$ the random forest's relative cost is \PHCcRFFive{} and at $R=20$ it is \PHCcRFTwenty. Models whose validation-chosen thresholds still miss event seconds on the test folds rise above 1 instead, because each miss is weighted by $R$: at $R=20$ the relative cost is \PHCcEDDQNTwenty{} for E-DDQN and \PHCcDQNTwenty{} for the standard DQN. As point estimates, the random forest stays below the trivial policies at all \PHCcNratios{} ratios and E-DDQN at \PHCcEDDQNBelow{} of them; at $R=5$, with linkage groups as the unit of inference, neither model's difference from always alarming is significant after Holm adjustment (Section~\ref{sec:stats}). The negative safety result is therefore not an artifact of the particular ratio: over all models and ratios the lowest relative cost is \PHCcMinAll{} (random forest at $R=\PHCcMinAllRatio$), so no model removes more than about \PHCcMinAllReduction\,\% of the cost of the better trivial policy at any ratio.

\subsection{Statistical Comparison}
\label{sec:stats}
Table~\ref{tab:paired} compares E-DDQN with every other primary model on identical test samples (seed ensembles, rule D1). Sample-level McNemar tests give $p<0.001$ for every pair, but they treat \nSamplesEval{} strongly autocorrelated seconds as independent and overstate the evidence; they are reported for completeness, with the contingency identity $b-c=\mathrm{err}_B-\mathrm{err}_A$ checked and recomputed with an independent library. The primary inference is the group bootstrap, which resamples the \nGroupsEval{} independent units. E-DDQN's seed-ensemble cost (\PHEnsEDDQNCost) exceeds that of every other primary model by \PairDOneECBCostDiff{} to \PairDOneRFCostDiff. These ensemble differences are larger than the corresponding differences of seed means in Table~\ref{tab:main} (\PHMeanDiffDQN{} to \PHMeanDiffRF) because E-DDQN's ensemble is worse than its average seed (Section~\ref{sec:seeds}); after Holm adjustment over the six comparisons, only the difference from the random forest remains significant under the group bootstrap (\PairDOneRFCostDiff, 95\,\% CI \PairDOneRFCostLo{} to \PairDOneRFCostHi; adjusted $p=$\,\PHHolmRF), whereas the adjusted $p$-values for the other five comparisons lie between \PHHolmECB{} and \PHHolmHGB. In particular, the difference from the standard DQN (\PairDOneDQNCostDiff; seed means \PHMeanDiffDQN) is not significant (unadjusted $p=$\,\PairDOneDQNPboot).

The direction of these conclusions is robust to the resampling unit and to individual groups, but the strength of the evidence depends on the test. Resampling the \nSessionsEval{} sessions instead of the groups gives the same pattern (E-DDQN vs.\ random forest: CI \PHSesRFLow{} to \PHSesRFHigh, Holm-adjusted $p=$\,\PHSesRFHolm). In a leave-one-group-out jackknife over the \PHJkGroups{} groups, the cost difference between E-DDQN and the random forest ranges from \PHJkEDDQNvsRFMin{} to \PHJkEDDQNvsRFMax{} and never changes sign; the same holds for every other comparison with E-DDQN. The exact group sign-flip test, which does not rely on a bootstrap approximation with \nGroupsEval{} clusters~\cite{cameron2008bootstrap}, is more conservative: it gives $p=$\,\PHSfRFP{} for E-DDQN versus the random forest (\PHSfRFHolm{} after Holm adjustment) and $p\ge$\,\PHSfECBP{} for the other five comparisons (Supplementary Sec.~S5.8). E-DDQN's higher cost than the random forest is therefore consistent across folds, groups and resampling schemes, but it is not established at a family-wise 5\,\% level by every test. Compared with always alarming, the random forest's seed-ensemble cost differs by \PHAlwRFGrp{} (CI \PHAlwRFGrpLow{} to \PHAlwRFGrpHigh; unadjusted $p=$\,\PHAlwRFGrpP), but after Holm adjustment over the nine models this is not significant ($p=$\,\PHAlwRFGrpHolm; sign-flip test $p=$\,\PHSfAlwaysRFP, adjusted \PHSfAlwaysRFHolm); E-DDQN's seed-ensemble cost exceeds that of always alarming by \PHAlwEDDQNGrp{} (CI \PHAlwEDDQNGrpLow{} to \PHAlwEDDQNGrpHigh; unadjusted $p=$\,\PHAlwEDDQNGrpP), which is not significant either: E-DDQN is not shown to be worse than the trivial policy, and, with linkage groups as the unit of inference, no model is shown to improve on it at the 5:1 ratio. Only when the \nSessionsEval{} sessions rather than the groups are resampled, which ignores possible dependence between linked sessions, does the random forest's advantage remain significant after Holm adjustment ($p=$\,\PHAlwRFSesP, adjusted \PHAlwRFSesHolm, approximate). All comparisons in this subsection use E-DDQN's primary search draw; Section~\ref{sec:why} reports how its comparison with the standard DQN changes with the draw.

\subsection{Event-Level Behavior}
At the cost-optimal operating points (seed ensembles) every model flags almost every session. Averaged over sessions, E-DDQN raises an alarm on \EDDQNEvFreeAlarm\,\% of the seconds in the \nEventFreeSessions{} sessions in which no discomfort was ever reported and on \EDDQNEvPreAlarm\,\% of the seconds before onset in the \nEventSessions{} sessions with an event (random forest: \RFEvFreeAlarm\,\% and \RFEvPreAlarm\,\%). Events are therefore ``detected'' with zero median delay by construction, not through discrimination.

\subsection{Why E-DDQN Under-Performs in the Primary Analysis}
\label{sec:why}
Validation selected $\gamma=$ \EDDQNGammas{} for the five outer folds of E-DDQN, i.e.\ strong bootstrapping, and the exposure+profile feature set, which includes the questionnaire indicators, in \EDDQNnProfile{} of 5 folds. Three exploratory diagnostics and one experiment specified before it was run bear on why E-DDQN under-performs in the primary analysis.

\emph{(i) Bootstrapping.} Section~\ref{sec:formulation} shows that bootstrapping cannot improve the optimal policy here and only adds action-independent estimation noise to $\Delta Q$. The separately tuned $\gamma=0$ variant (E-CB) ranks better (ROC-AUC \ECBCostRocAucMean{} vs.\ \EDDQNCostRocAucMean; its cost difference from E-DDQN is not significant after Holm adjustment, Section~\ref{sec:stats}), but it also selected different feature sets (no questionnaire indicators in \PHECBNoProfileFolds{} of 5 folds; Supplementary Sec.~S2.2) and different networks, so this comparison does not isolate $\gamma$. With E-DDQN's configuration held fixed, setting $\gamma=0$ raises ROC-AUC only from \EDDQNCostRocAucMean{} to \AblGammaZeroCostRocAuc{} and changes the cost by less than one seed standard deviation (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\emph{(ii) Questionnaire indicators.} Across the seven primary models, the \PHFsProfile{} of \PHFsTotal{} selected configurations (model--fold pairs) whose feature set includes the questionnaire indicators have a lower within-fold ROC-AUC than the \PHFsNoProfile{} that do not (mean deviation from the fold mean \PHFsDevProfile{} vs.\ \PHFsDevNoProfile; without E-DDQN, \PHFsDevProfileExE{} vs.\ \PHFsDevNoProfileExE). In \PHFsFoldsSeparated{} of 5 outer folds every configuration with the indicators ranks below every configuration without them (Supplementary Sec.~S5.9). A test group never shares its exact profile with the training groups, so these indicators can transfer only through shared attribute values; with \nGroupsEval{} groups they may instead let a model fit group-level label rates that do not generalize. The analysis is exploratory and observational---feature sets were selected, not assigned---so it suggests, but does not establish, a cause.

\emph{(iii) Search draw and selection optimism.} Each family's random configurations (\PHDrawTotalEDDQN; 12 for logistic regression) were drawn once and reused in every outer fold. For E-DDQN this draw contained only \PHDrawExposureEDDQN{} configurations with the exposure feature set without questionnaire indicators (E-CB \PHDrawExposureECB, DQN \PHDrawExposureDQN) and \PHDrawQEDDQN{} with the indicators (Supplementary Sec.~S5.10). On validation data the best E-DDQN candidate with the indicators had the lower cost in \PHDrawFoldsQLowerEDDQN{} of 5 folds (e.g.\ \PHDrawFoldZeroQEDDQN{} vs.\ \PHDrawFoldZeroNoQEDDQN{} in fold~0), so the selection rule chose it each time. Experiment (iv) tests how much E-DDQN's result depends on which configurations its random draw happened to offer. Table~\ref{tab:optimism} compares, per family, the inner-validation cost of the configuration at the moment it was selected with its outer-test cost. The gap is \EDDQNOptGap{} for E-DDQN but between \RFOptGap{} and \ECBOptGap{} for all other models. The selection criterion, the lowest achievable 5:1 cost on the inner out-of-fold predictions, is nearly flat near the always-alarm regime (\PHSelCostMin--\PHSelCostMax{} for the selected configurations, against \AlwaysCost{} for always alarming). Combined with noisy, high-variance training curves and validation checkpointing, it lets configurations look best on validation folds by chance~\cite{varma2006bias}.

\emph{(iv) Search-draw experiment.} Specified, with three decision rules, and committed before any of its models was trained (Supplementary Sec.~S5.11), this experiment tuned E-DDQN and the standard DQN again under the full protocol with two further random draws of 16 configurations each and with one draw restricted to the exposure feature set. In no draw did E-DDQN have a significantly lower cost than the standard DQN (seed-ensemble differences \SDCostDiffMin{} to \SDCostDiffMax; smallest group-bootstrap $p=$\,\SDCostPMin), so the conclusion of Section~\ref{sec:main} is not challenged (rule R1). E-DDQN's own result, however, depended strongly on the draw (rule R2): its seed-mean cost ranged from \SDEDDQNCostMin{} to \SDEDDQNCostMax{} and its within-fold ROC-AUC from \SDEDDQNWfMin, the lowest of the primary models, to \SDEDDQNWfMax, close to the highest (random forest, \PHWfRFAuc); the standard DQN varied less (cost \SDDQNCostMin--\SDDQNCostMax, within-fold ROC-AUC \SDDQNWfMin--\SDDQNWfMax). The ranking of the two agents reversed between draws: the standard DQN ranked seconds better in draw~1 (seed-ensemble ROC-AUC difference \SDPairOneAucDiff, CI \SDPairOneAucLow{} to \SDPairOneAucHigh, $p=$\,\SDPairOneAucP, approximate) and E-DDQN in draw~2 (\SDPairTwoAucDiff, CI \SDPairTwoAucLow{} to \SDPairTwoAucHigh, $p=$\,\SDPairTwoAucP). E-DDQN's further draws offered \SDEDDQNDrawTwoExpCand{} and \SDEDDQNDrawThreeExpCand{} exposure-only candidates, against \SDEDDQNDrawOneExpCand{} in draw~1, and selected the questionnaire indicators in \SDEDDQNDrawTwoQFolds{} and \SDEDDQNDrawThreeQFolds{} of 5 folds, against \SDEDDQNDrawOneQFolds. Restricted to the exposure features, E-DDQN's within-fold ROC-AUC (\SDEDDQNExposureWfAuc) was within \SDExposureGap{} of the standard DQN's (\SDDQNExposureWfAuc) and their cost difference was not significant (\SDPairExposureDiff, CI \SDPairExposureLow{} to \SDPairExposureHigh), which supports the feature-set explanation (rule R3). In draw~2, E-DDQN selected $\gamma=0.99$ in \SDEDDQNDrawTwoStrongGammaFolds{} of 5 folds and still reached its highest within-fold ROC-AUC, so strong bootstrapping did not by itself prevent good ranking (descriptive).

Together, the diagnostics and the experiment indicate that E-DDQN's weak primary result is largely a consequence of its search draw---a draw dominated by questionnaire-based configurations, combined with noisy validation-based selection---rather than of its reward or of bootstrapping. The experiment varied the draw and the feature set, not single components, so it does not show whether any component helps a configuration that ranks well. Q-value estimates stay finite, so the primary result is not caused by numerical divergence, but they are poorly scaled. The largest magnitude over all E-DDQN runs is \EDDQNQAbsMax{} (DQN \DQNQAbsMax, E-CB \ECBQAbsMax), and relative to the return bound $r_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$ of each fold's selected discount the largest estimates exceed the bound by up to a factor of \PHQRatioMaxEDDQN{} for E-DDQN (in \PHQRatioFoldsEDDQN{} of 5 folds), \PHQRatioMaxDQN{} for DQN and \PHQRatioMaxECB{} for E-CB, i.e.\ extrapolation on atypical states. The mean greedy-action Q-value differs from its realized discounted validation return by \EDDQNOverest{} for E-DDQN, \DQNOverest{} for DQN and \ECBOverest{} for E-CB; for E-DDQN and E-CB, this comparison is also affected by the class mix of the replay, which differs from that of the validation data (Section~\ref{sec:formulation}).

\subsection{Sanity Check: Telemetry Perturbations (RQ3)}
\label{sec:attack}
Table~\ref{tab:attack-auc} reports threshold-free ranking under the whole-session perturbations. \AttAucFracSmall\,\% of the model--scenario combinations change the pooled ROC-AUC by less than 0.03 and none by more than \AttAucMaxAbsChange{} (largest drops: acceleration-spike injection in E-CB and DQN, speed down-scaling in gradient boosting). This insensitivity is not evidence of a robust safety monitor. Of the \PHFsTotal{} selected configurations, \PHFsExposure{} use the exposure feature sets, in which elapsed time and the game indicator are untouched by every perturbation and the remaining inputs are averages or cumulative sums over many seconds; a perturbation applied to every test session alike can, moreover, shift the scores without reordering them; and the primary E-DDQN's ranking is not distinguishable from chance in any case (Section~\ref{sec:main}). Fixed thresholds are more informative: at the cost-optimal operating points (Supplementary Sec.~S4.3), the per-session worst-case evasion adversary lowers recall on event seconds by \AttEvasionDropMin--\AttEvasionDropMax{} percentage points (E-DDQN: from \EDDQNAttCleanRecall\,\% to \EDDQNAttWorstRecall\,\%), and the false-alarm adversary raises every model's false-positive rate to \EDDQNAttWorstFPR--100\,\% (\RFAttCleanFPR--\CSMLPAttCleanFPR\,\% for the seed ensembles without manipulation). These are lower bounds from a weak, candidate-selecting adversary on feature-space perturbations, not evidence about real rendering attacks.

\subsection{Explainability}
\label{sec:xai}
SHAP attributions of the decision margin $\Delta Q$ of the primary E-DDQN (Fig.~\ref{fig:shap}) are dominated by \ShapTopFive{} (telemetry features named as in the released feature dictionary, Supplementary Sec.~S1.4; questionnaire indicators with translated labels). The decisions are driven mainly by exposure time, cumulative exposure and questionnaire answers rather than by instantaneous telemetry, consistent with the exposure-time baselines and with diagnostic (ii) of Section~\ref{sec:why}. Because the primary E-DDQN's ranking is not distinguishable from chance (Section~\ref{sec:main}), these attributions describe what the model uses, not a validated signal. Because attributions are computed after the decision, they add no latency to detection.
