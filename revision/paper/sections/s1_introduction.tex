\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Immersive virtual reality (VR) is used in clinical rehabilitation, industrial and defense training and human-performance assessment, where the headset shapes perception, balance and spatial orientation rather than merely presenting information. Cybersickness---nausea, disorientation, oculomotor strain and, in severe cases, postural instability---is a long-standing side effect of the sensory conflict that VR creates~\cite{caserman2021cybersickness}. It can also be induced deliberately: an adversary who manipulates rendering parameters such as frame pacing, camera motion or field of view could make users sick or unsafe~\cite{tseng2022dark,odeleye2021framerate}, and attacks on the inputs of cybersickness detectors can stop a mitigation from being triggered~\cite{kundu2025securing}. In both cases a detector that recognizes cybersickness during immersion, so that a mitigation can be triggered, is a safety function, and its two errors are not equally costly: a missed event leaves the user exposed to harm, whereas a false alarm causes a brief, recoverable interruption.

Detectors have progressed from post-exposure questionnaires to deep temporal and multimodal models of physiological, eye-tracking and gameplay data~\cite{dennison2016physio,islam2020automatic,islam2021fusion,liu2024cnneca,yalcin2024automatic,jeong2024precyse}. To encode unequal error costs, several authors have cast classification as a sequential decision problem for reinforcement learning (RL) and trained deep Q-networks (DQNs) with class-dependent rewards~\cite{lin2020imbalanced,yang2024drlimbalanced}. E-DDQN follows this line for cybersickness: a dueling Double DQN~\cite{vanhasselt2016double,wang2016dueling} whose reward penalizes a missed event five times more than a false alarm, trained with class-balanced replay and soft target updates. Unlike~\cite{lin2020imbalanced,yang2024drlimbalanced}, it does not end an episode when an event is missed; episodes end only with the recorded session.

Two questions must be answered before such a detector can be credited with a safety benefit. The first concerns evaluation. Gameplay telemetry is logged about once per second and self-reported discomfort changes rarely within a session, so any split that places seconds of the same session or participant in both training and test data rewards recognition of the session rather than detection of cybersickness. Oversampling before splitting is a known source of such leakage~\cite{kapoor2023leakage}, and published results on the gameplay dataset used here were obtained with a 70/30 split of the data for which no participant- or session-level separation is reported~\cite{kundu2022truvr}. An earlier, unpublished version of the present study reported \pct{96.88} accuracy for E-DDQN under an oversampling-before-splitting protocol. The second question concerns the formulation. It is rarely made explicit what an asymmetric reward adds to classical cost-sensitive learning~\cite{elkan2001cost}, and RL detectors are seldom compared with cost-weighted supervised models tuned and thresholded under the same rules.

We therefore address three research questions on the public UFFCSData dataset~\cite{porcino2019uffcsdata}:
\begin{itemize}
\item[RQ1] What does the asymmetric reward add, formally, to cost-sensitive classification?
\item[RQ2] Under leakage-controlled, session-grouped evaluation, does E-DDQN outperform a standard DQN, cost-weighted supervised models and a minimal exposure-time baseline, and does any model meet a safety-prioritized operating target?
\item[RQ3] As a secondary sanity check of what the detectors rely on: how sensitive are they to bounded manipulation of the telemetry channels that an adversary could influence?
\end{itemize}
RQ3 is a feature-space stress test, not an evaluation of attacks: UFFCSData contains no manipulated sessions, so it cannot show whether a detector recognizes real rendering attacks or is robust to them.

The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Formal link between RL and cost-sensitive learning.} Applying known results on cost-sensitive classification and contextual bandits~\cite{elkan2001cost,dudik2011efficient}, we make explicit that, on logged data, the alarm cannot change future observations, so detection is a contextual bandit, and that the 5:1 reward induces exactly the Bayes decision rule $P(y=1\mid s)\ge1/6$ of 5:1 cost-sensitive classification; we also show how error-triggered episode termination, used in earlier DQN classifiers, departs from this setting, and that class-balanced replay shifts the operating point actually learned (Section~\ref{sec:formulation}).
\item \emph{A matched, leakage-controlled benchmark.} We compare E-DDQN with a standard DQN, a contextual-bandit variant and four cost-weighted supervised models under a nested, session-grouped protocol fixed before testing, with the same search procedure and the same number of configurations (16 per model; 12 for logistic regression; the search spaces differ greatly in size), the same checkpoint and threshold rules and paired statistics; two exposure-time baselines were added post hoc under the same protocol (Sections~\ref{sec:groups}--\ref{sec:protocol}). Session grouping is required on these data: random row-level splits, with or without oversampling, give ROC-AUC \LeakRowRocAuc--\LeakOldRocAuc, against \LeakGrpRocAuc{} under session-grouped splits, which quantifies a known pitfall~\cite{kapoor2023leakage,kaufman2012leakage} on this dataset; a reconstruction of the earlier version's protocol yields similarly inflated scores (Section~\ref{sec:leak}).
\item \emph{Findings.} E-DDQN does not have a significantly lower 5:1 cost than the standard DQN, with its primary search draw or with two further draws specified before they were run, and in ranking the standard DQN was better with the primary draw (unadjusted $p=$\,\SDPairOneAucP, approximate) and E-DDQN with another ($p=$\,\SDPairTwoAucP); with its primary draw its seed-ensemble cost is higher than that of a cost-weighted random forest in every outer fold (marginally in one), a difference that is significant under the primary group bootstrap but not under an exact sign-flip sensitivity test after Holm adjustment and is therefore not firmly established; in a post-hoc analysis, no primary model ranks seconds significantly better than elapsed time alone; and no model combines high recall with a controlled false-alarm rate (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item \emph{Diagnosis and stress tests.} The results suggest that sensitivity to the random search draw contributes substantially to E-DDQN's weak primary result: its primary draw was dominated by configurations that use the questionnaire answers, across three draws its within-fold ROC-AUC ranged from \SDEDDQNWfMin, the lowest of the primary models, to \SDEDDQNWfMax, close to the highest, and restricted to exposure features it ranked seconds about as well as the standard DQN; draws, feature sets and other hyper-parameters varied together, so no single cause is isolated (Section~\ref{sec:why}); in a secondary feature-space stress test, bounded telemetry perturbations change \AttAucFracSmall\,\% of the detectors' scenario-wise ROC-AUC values by less than 0.03 (at most \AttAucMaxAbsChange), which is not evidence of robustness, whereas an adaptive adversary still changes decisions at fixed thresholds (Section~\ref{sec:attack}).
\item \emph{Reproducibility.} Code, the fixed protocol and per-sample predictions are released so that every number in the paper can be regenerated from the raw data.
\end{enumerate}
