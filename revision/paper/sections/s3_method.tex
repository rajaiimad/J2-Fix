\section{Methods}
\label{sec:method}

The methodology has nine steps (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Steps~1--3 build the data so that no information can pass from test to training data: rebuild the data and define the target (Section~\ref{sec:data}), make every split at the level of independent session groups before anything is learned (Section~\ref{sec:groups}), and compute causal features with preprocessing fitted on training data only (Section~\ref{sec:features}). Step~4 states formally what the asymmetric reward means (Section~\ref{sec:formulation}). Steps~5 and~6 define E-DDQN and its matched baselines (Sections~\ref{sec:eddqn} and~\ref{sec:baselines}). Step~7 tunes, thresholds and compares every model with one protocol that uses validation data only and was fixed before testing (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). Steps~8 and~9 stress-test and explain the trained detectors (Sections~\ref{sec:threat} and~\ref{sec:shap}). Table~\ref{tab:notation} defines the main terms.

\subsection{Step 1: Data and Target}
\label{sec:data}
\emph{Dataset.} UFFCSData~\cite{porcino2019uffcsdata,porcino2022identifying,porcino2021methodology} was recorded while volunteers played a race game and a flight game in a head-mounted display. About once per second the game logged the player's speed and acceleration, three camera-rotation components (X, Y and Z; their range $[-1,1]$ is consistent with the vector part of a unit quaternion, but the documentation does not state the parameterization), the frame rate, the region of interest, session-configuration flags and the participant's self-reported discomfort level on a 0--3 scale (none, slight, moderate, severe) based on the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire~\cite{kennedy1993ssq,porcino2019demo}. Each session also stores the participant's answers to a Cybersickness Profile Questionnaire (CSPQ), completed before playing: gender, age band, VR experience, glasses, vision problems, eye dominance and flicker sensitivity. Two further profile fields, a symptoms field and posture, are constant in the curated release.

\emph{Provenance.} We rebuilt every input from the original XML exports rather than from derived files. The dataset authors' curated release (\nSessionsEval{} seated sessions recorded in December~2019; \nSamplesEval{} samples) matches \nSessionsEval{} of the raw exports sample by sample and is our evaluation set. The \nSessionsDev{} standing-posture sessions recorded in October~2019 are used \emph{only} to debug code and calibrate training budgets; two further December sessions that the dataset authors excluded are not used.

\emph{Target.} The binary target is $y_t=1$ (\emph{cybersickness event}) if the reported discomfort level at second $t$ is at least~1, and $y_t=0$ otherwise. The prevalence is \prevEval{} (Flight \prevFlight{}, Race \prevRace{}). Grades 2--3 occur in \PHGradeRaceSevere{} of \PHGradeRaceSamples{} Race samples but in only \PHGradeFlightSevere{} of \PHGradeFlightSamples{} Flight samples. The discomfort level is piecewise constant within a session: it changes on average \PHLabelGradeChanges{} times per session, at irregular times, and the binary label \PHLabelBinaryChanges{} times; it never decreases in \PHLabelNonDecreasing{} of the \PHLabelSessions{} sessions, and \nEventFreeSessions{} sessions never report discomfort. How and how often participants were prompted to report during play is not specified in the public documentation available to us, so consecutive labels should be read as a held self-report rather than as independent per-second measurements. We stress that $y$ marks self-reported discomfort, \emph{not} the presence of an attack: no session in the dataset was attacked.

\subsection{Step 2: Unit of Independence and Nested Grouped Splits}
\label{sec:groups}
\emph{Why grouping is needed.} Consecutive seconds of a session have almost identical features and usually the same label. If seconds of one session appeared in both training and test data, a model could score well by recognizing the session rather than by detecting cybersickness; Section~\ref{sec:leak} quantifies this effect. Every split must therefore keep each session---and, as far as the data allow, each participant---on one side.

\emph{Session-linkage groups.} The release contains no participant identifier, and sessions are not independent: some participants restarted after an aborted session (e.g., a 104-s session followed 108\,s later by a full session with an identical nine-field profile). We therefore link two sessions if (a)~their nine profile fields are identical, or (b)~one starts within 180\,s after an aborted session (fewer than 200 samples) and their profiles differ in at most two fields. The connected components of these links are the \emph{session-linkage groups}; there are \nGroupsEval{} of them. The rule may merge different people who share a profile, which only makes the evaluation more conservative, but it cannot detect one person who answered the questionnaire differently in non-consecutive sessions; we discuss this residual risk in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\emph{Nested grouped cross-validation.} Every split assigns whole groups. The \emph{outer} loop divides the groups into \nOuter{} folds; each fold serves once as test data, and the remaining groups form that fold's \emph{development set}. The \emph{inner} loop divides each development set into \nInner{} folds: a model is trained on two inner folds and validated on the third, so that every development second receives exactly one out-of-fold (OOF) prediction. Inner validation data are used for hyper-parameter search, checkpoint selection and threshold selection; the outer-test fold is used only for the final evaluation. Folds are formed by stratified group $k$-fold splitting on game~$\times$~label (outer seed 20261012; inner seeds 20261013--20261017). Every sample of the evaluation set is therefore predicted exactly once, by models that never saw its session, its linkage group or its exact questionnaire profile. Executable checks confirm zero session, group and profile overlap and zero duplicated feature rows between every development set and its test fold.

\subsection{Step 3: Causal Features and Train-Only Preprocessing}
\label{sec:features}
A feature is \emph{causal} if its value at second $t$ uses only seconds $0,\dots,t$ of the same session, as a monitor running during play would; we verified this by recomputing all features on sessions truncated at random times. Writing $q_t$ for the three rotation components, the 39 telemetry features are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{instantaneous} (13): game indicator, elapsed time, speed, acceleration, $|$acceleration$|$, the three components of $q_t$, the per-second rotation change $\lVert q_t-q_{t-1}\rVert$, the frame rate and three region-of-interest indicators;
\item \emph{trailing windows} (22): over the last 5\,s and the last 15\,s (including $t$; shorter at the start of a session), the mean, standard deviation and change of speed, the mean and maximum of $|$acceleration$|$, the acceleration-spike rate, the mean and standard deviation of the rotation change, and the mean, minimum and frame-drop fraction of the frame rate;
\item \emph{cumulative} (4): cumulative rotation change, cumulative numbers of spikes and frame drops, and the running mean speed.
\end{itemize}
A second is an \emph{acceleration spike} if $|$acceleration$|$ exceeds the 90th percentile of the training rows, and a \emph{frame drop} if the frame rate is below 80\,fps (more than 11\,\% below the 90-Hz display target). The logger writes a frame rate of 0 on the very first sample of some sessions; this artifact is replaced by the training median. In addition, 11 CSPQ indicators encode the seven questionnaire items (one indicator per non-reference answer, and one indicator per condition for vision problems).

All learned preprocessing---the spike threshold, the imputation value and a median/IQR (robust) scaler---is fitted on the training rows of each inner split only and then applied unchanged to its validation and test rows. Columns that carry no usable information were removed: the field of view and the static-frame and auto-camera flags are constant within each game (pure game indicators), four configuration flags and posture are constant overall, and the absolute player position was excluded because it encodes elapsed course distance and is absent in the October wave. No oversampling or synthetic data are used anywhere; class imbalance is handled only inside training (Sections~\ref{sec:eddqn} and~\ref{sec:baselines}).

\emph{Feature sets.} Which features a model sees is a hyper-parameter chosen on validation folds, from four sets: \emph{telemetry} (39 features), \emph{telemetry+profile} (50), \emph{exposure} (8 exposure descriptors fixed a priori: game, elapsed time, cumulative rotation change, cumulative spikes, running and 15-s mean speed, and 15-s means of the rotation change and of $|$acceleration$|$) and \emph{exposure+profile} (19). Two further sets, \emph{time} (elapsed time only) and \emph{time+game}, are used only by the post-hoc baselines of Section~\ref{sec:baselines}.

\subsection{Step 4: Decision Formulation---MDP or Contextual Bandit?}
\label{sec:formulation}
\emph{Decision process.} As in DQN-based detection~\cite{lin2020imbalanced,yang2024drlimbalanced}, each session is an episode. At second $t$ the detector observes the state $s_t\in\mathbb{R}^d$ (the causal feature vector), chooses an action $a_t\in\{0,1\}$ (stay silent or raise an alarm) and receives the reward $r_t=R(a_t,y_t)$ with
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
R(1,1)&=+0.5, & R(0,0)&=+0.1,\\
R(0,1)&=-0.5, & R(1,0)&=-0.1.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
The next state $s_{t+1}$ is the next second \emph{of the same session}. The last second of a session is terminal ($d_t=1$; otherwise $d_t=0$), so no transition links two sessions.

\emph{Key observation.} The telemetry was logged while participants played, with no alarm in the loop, so the action cannot change what is observed next: $P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)=P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t)$. For any discount factor $\gamma<1$, the optimal action-value function therefore splits into an immediate term and a term that does not depend on the action:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
Q^*(s,a)={}&\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[R(a,y_t)\mid s_t=s]}_{\text{immediate reward}}\\
&+\underbrace{\gamma\,\mathbb{E}[(1-d_t)\,V^*(s_{t+1})\mid s_t=s]}_{\text{same for both actions}}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
Three consequences follow.

\emph{(i) The problem is a contextual bandit.} Because the second term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:decomp} is the same for both actions, the best action at every second is the one with the higher expected immediate reward, whatever the value of $\gamma$. The logged-data problem is therefore a \emph{contextual bandit}~\cite{lattimore2020bandit}---a series of one-step decisions, each based on an observed context, whose actions do not affect later contexts---rather than a control problem in which actions steer future states.

\emph{(ii) The reward fixes a cost-sensitive threshold.} With $p(s)=P(y=1\mid s)$, the decision margin of the optimal Q-function is
\begin{equation}
Q^*(s,1)-Q^*(s,0)=p(s)\,\Delta_{\mathrm{FN}}-(1-p(s))\,\Delta_{\mathrm{FP}},
\label{eq:margin}
\end{equation}
where the \emph{regrets}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\Delta_{\mathrm{FN}}&=R(1,1)-R(0,1)=1.0,\\
\Delta_{\mathrm{FP}}&=R(0,0)-R(1,0)=0.2
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:regret}
\end{equation}
are the reward lost by missing an event and by raising a false alarm. The greedy policy alarms if and only if the margin is non-negative, i.e. if $p(s)\ge \Delta_{\mathrm{FP}}/(\Delta_{\mathrm{FN}}+\Delta_{\mathrm{FP}})=1/6$. This is exactly the Bayes-optimal decision of a classifier with misclassification costs $C_{\mathrm{FN}}:C_{\mathrm{FP}}=5:1$~\cite{elkan2001cost}, which alarms when the expected cost of staying silent, $5p$, is at least the expected cost of an alarm, $1-p$. The asymmetric reward is therefore not a different objective from cost-sensitive learning: it encodes the same 5:1 operating point.

\emph{(iii) All models can share one score scale.} Solving Eq.~\eqref{eq:margin} for $p$ turns a learned Q-function into an implied probability,
\begin{equation}
\hat p(s)=\frac{\Delta Q(s)+\Delta_{\mathrm{FP}}}{\Delta_{\mathrm{FN}}+\Delta_{\mathrm{FP}}},\qquad \Delta Q(s)=Q(s,1)-Q(s,0),
\label{eq:phat}
\end{equation}
computed with each agent's own reward table (for the symmetric $\pm1$ reward of the standard DQN, $\Delta_{\mathrm{FN}}=\Delta_{\mathrm{FP}}=2$). For a learned, imperfect Q-function, $\hat p$ is only an estimate: it is not calibrated by construction and can fall outside $[0,1]$. Cost-weighted classifiers are mapped to the same scale by Eq.~\eqref{eq:unweight} (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}), so the Bayes cut-off is $1/6$ for every model and thresholds can be compared directly. Consequences (i)--(iii) follow from standard results---the cost-sensitive Bayes threshold~\cite{elkan2001cost} and the equivalence of a contextual bandit with full reward information to cost-sensitive classification~\cite{dudik2011efficient}; we state them because asymmetric-reward DQN detectors are usually presented without this link.

\emph{Error-triggered termination.} Earlier DQN classifiers end an episode when a minority-class sample is misclassified~\cite{lin2020imbalanced,yang2024drlimbalanced}. There the action does change the future: a missed event ends the reward stream, so the problem is no longer a contextual bandit. With $\gamma>0$ a miss then also forfeits the discounted value of continuing, $\gamma\,V(s_{t+1})$, which acts as an additional, $\gamma$-dependent penalty on that error; the implied threshold therefore depends on $\gamma$ and on the value of the remaining episode, not on the reward table alone. E-DDQN does not use error-triggered termination: its episodes end only with the recorded session, so Eqs.~\eqref{eq:decomp}--\eqref{eq:phat} apply to it exactly.

\emph{Role of the discount factor.} Bootstrapping with $\gamma>0$ adds the action-independent second term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:decomp}, whose estimation noise enters both Q-values; it cannot improve the optimal policy and can help or hurt only through estimation effects. We therefore treat $\gamma\in\{0,0.5,0.9,0.99\}$ as a hyper-parameter chosen on validation data and also evaluate the special case $\gamma=0$ as a separately tuned model (E-CB). A genuine MDP would require the alarm to change later rendering or symptoms (e.g., a mitigation in the loop); no such intervention exists in the data, so we make no claim that E-DDQN learns adaptive sequential control.

\subsection{Step 5: E-DDQN}
\label{sec:eddqn}
E-DDQN combines the asymmetric reward of Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward} with standard deep-RL components. Each component is listed with its purpose and its implementation.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Network.} A multilayer trunk (width and depth tuned; every hidden layer is linear, followed by batch or layer normalization~\cite{ioffe2015bn}, ReLU and, except after the last hidden layer, dropout~\cite{srivastava2014dropout}) feeds a dueling head~\cite{wang2016dueling}, which separates a state value $V(s)$ from action advantages $A(s,a)$: $Q(s,a)=V(s)+A(s,a)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a'}A(s,a')$. The Q-values are not squashed.
\item \emph{Double-DQN target.} To reduce the over-estimation caused by the max operator~\cite{vanhasselt2016double}, the online network $\theta$ chooses the next action and a target network $\theta^-$ evaluates it:
\begin{equation}
y_t=r_t+\gamma(1-d_t)\,Q_{\theta^-}\big(s_{t+1},\arg\max_{a'}Q_\theta(s_{t+1},a')\big),
\label{eq:ddqn}
\end{equation}
where the arg-max is computed with the online network in evaluation mode; for $\gamma=0$, $y_t=r_t$. The Huber loss~\cite{girshick2015fast} between $Q_\theta(s_t,a_t)$ and $y_t$ is minimized with Adam~\cite{kingma2015adam} and $\ell_2$ weight decay, gradient-norm clipping at 1.0 and a cosine learning-rate schedule~\cite{loshchilov2017sgdr} that decays to $10^{-5}$ over the training epochs.
\item \emph{Soft target updates.} After every gradient update, $\theta^-\leftarrow(1-\tau)\theta^-+\tau\theta$ (Polyak averaging~\cite{lillicrap2016continuous}); the batch-normalization running statistics are copied to the target network as well.
\item \emph{Class-balanced replay.} Transitions are stored in a circular buffer~\cite{lin1992selfimproving} of 50,000 transitions (doubled when full-information replay is used); every minibatch contains a fixed fraction $\rho$ of non-event and $1-\rho$ of event transitions, so that neither class dominates the updates.
\item \emph{Exploration and warm-up.} Actions are $\varepsilon$-greedy; $\varepsilon$ starts at 1.0 and is multiplied by a tuned decay factor after every epoch, down to 0.01. Optionally, the buffer is first filled with 4,000 transitions with uniformly random actions.
\item \emph{Full-information replay (tuned on/off).} Offline, the label reveals the reward of \emph{both} actions. E-DDQN may therefore also store the action that was not taken, together with \emph{its own} reward. This is a valid transition, and it turns Q-learning into cost-sensitive regression of $\mathbb{E}[R\mid s,a]$.
\item \emph{Validation checkpointing.} Every second epoch the agent scores its inner-validation groups, and the epoch with the lowest achievable 5:1 cost (the minimum over thresholds; ties: higher PR-AUC) is kept.
\end{itemize}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:eddqn} summarizes training.

\emph{Implementation pitfalls.} Three errors are easy to make when a classifier is trained as a Q-learner, and all three were present in an earlier implementation of this study: (i)~building transitions across session boundaries, so that the next state of a session's last second is the first second of another session; (ii)~storing the true label as the action while computing the reward of a different, exploratory action (a form of teacher forcing that trains the correct action toward the penalty of the wrong one); and (iii)~averaging only the parameters into the target network, so that its batch-normalization statistics never leave their initial values. Executable unit tests guard against each of them and also verify the 5:1 regret ratio and bounded Q-values.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{E-DDQN training for one inner split}
\label{alg:eddqn}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\small
\State Initialize $\theta$, $\theta^-\!\leftarrow\theta$, buffer $\mathcal{D}$; optional warm-up with random actions on within-session transitions
\For{epoch $=1,\dots,E$}
  \State Concatenate training sessions in random order, each in temporal order; mark the last second of each session terminal
  \For{each chunk of 256 consecutive transitions}
    \State $a_t\gets$ $\varepsilon$-greedy on $Q_\theta(s_t,\cdot)$;\ $r_t\gets R(a_t,y_t)$
    \State store $(s_t,a_t,r_t,s_{t+1},d_t,y_t)$; if full-information replay, also store $(s_t,1-a_t,R(1-a_t,y_t),s_{t+1},d_t,y_t)$
    \State every 2 chunks: $k\in\{1,2,4\}$ (tuned) updates on class-balanced minibatches with target \eqref{eq:ddqn} and Huber loss; after each update, Polyak update of $\theta^-$ and copy of BN statistics
  \EndFor
  \State 5 consolidation updates;\ decay $\varepsilon$;\ cosine LR step
  \State every 2 epochs: score inner-validation groups; keep $\theta$ if the achievable 5:1 cost improves
\EndFor
\State \Return checkpoint; score $\hat p(s)$ by Eq.~\eqref{eq:phat}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Step 6: Matched Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}
Every baseline receives the same splits, candidate feature sets, target, random-search procedure (16 configurations; 12 for logistic regression, whose search space contains only 24 distinct configurations), checkpoint rule (neural models), cross-fitted training recipe and threshold rules as E-DDQN. The number of configurations is matched, but not the coverage of the search spaces, which differ greatly in size (Section~\ref{sec:setup}).
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Standard DQN}~\cite{mnih2015human}: symmetric $\pm1$ reward, plain (non-dueling) head, single max-operator target $r_t+\gamma(1-d_t)\max_{a'}Q_{\theta^-}(s_{t+1},a')$, hard target copies every 10, 100 or 500 updates (tuned), uniform replay and no full-information replay; it uses the same episodes, transitions, exploration and warm-up options, and $\gamma$ is tuned as for E-DDQN.
\item \textbf{E-CB}: E-DDQN with $\gamma=0$, i.e.\ a contextual-bandit learner; the target network and the double estimator are then inactive by construction.
\item \textbf{CS-MLP}: a cost-sensitive multilayer perceptron with the same trunk family and a positive-class-weighted cross-entropy ($w=5$, i.e.\ the same 5:1 cost), trained on uniform or class-balanced minibatches and checkpointed like the agents.
\item \textbf{Logistic regression}, \textbf{histogram gradient boosting (HGB)} and \textbf{random forest (RF)}~\cite{pedregosa2011sklearn}, each with class weight 5 for event seconds.
\item Two trivial references: \emph{always alarm} and \emph{never alarm}.
\end{itemize}
\emph{One score scale for weighted models.} With event weight $w=5$, a weighted classifier estimates $p_w=5p/(5p+1-p)$ instead of $p=P(y=1\mid s)$. Inverting this relation gives
\begin{equation}
\hat p=\frac{p_w}{5-4p_w},
\label{eq:unweight}
\end{equation}
so that, together with Eq.~\eqref{eq:phat}, every model's score estimates $P(y=1\mid s)$ and the Bayes cut-off is $1/6$ for all of them.

\emph{Post-hoc exposure-time baselines.} Because exposure duration is a well-documented driver of cybersickness~\cite{kennedy2000duration}, the labels are close to step functions within a session, and the primary analysis selected exposure features for most models, two minimal baselines were specified after the primary analysis and run under exactly the same splits, selection, threshold rules and seeds: \textbf{Time-LR}, a class-weighted logistic regression on elapsed time only (all six values of $C$), and \textbf{Time+game HGB}, gradient boosting on elapsed time and the game indicator (same search space and budget as HGB). Both are reported separately from the primary comparison.

\subsection{Step 7: Evaluation Protocol and Statistics}
\label{sec:protocol}
The protocol was written and committed before any model was trained on the evaluation sessions; the post-hoc analyses (i)--(iv) listed at the end of this subsection were specified and committed after the primary results were known but before they were run, and analysis (v) was added after a pre-submission review, when all other results were known. For each model family and outer fold, the protocol has four stages, and the outer-test fold enters only in the last one.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Hyper-parameter search.} Each of the family's configurations (drawn once with a fixed seed) is trained on the three inner splits with one seed. The three validation predictions are pooled into OOF predictions for the whole development set, and the configuration with the lowest 5:1 cost $c=(5\,\mathrm{FN}+\mathrm{FP})/N$ at its cost-minimizing OOF threshold is selected (ties: higher PR-AUC).
\item \emph{Final training.} The selected configuration is trained again with five seeds. For each seed, the three inner models are kept, each checkpointed on its own validation fold (\emph{cross-fitting}): their validation predictions form the OOF scores, and the mean of their three scores is the outer-test score. The OOF and test scores therefore come from the same models.
\item \emph{Operating thresholds}, chosen on the OOF scores only. The primary rule \emph{D1} takes the threshold that minimizes the 5:1 cost (ties: the one closest to $1/6$). The secondary rules are \emph{D0}, the untuned Bayes cut-off $\hat p\ge1/6$ (for the asymmetric agents this is the greedy arg-max policy), and the largest threshold that reaches a validation recall of 0.95. Matched operating points use validation-chosen thresholds for recall targets of 0.80, 0.90, 0.95 and 0.99 and specificity targets of 0.5 and 0.8.
\item \emph{Single test evaluation.} The outer-test fold is scored once with the frozen models and thresholds, on clean telemetry and on the perturbed telemetry of Section~\ref{sec:threat}.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Metrics.} We report accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1, balanced accuracy, MCC~\cite{chicco2020mcc}, G-mean, false-positive and false-negative rates, missed events, the 5:1 cost, ROC-AUC and PR-AUC~\cite{saito2015pr}, exact confusion matrices, and mean~$\pm$~SD over the five seeds. Event-level metrics complement the per-second metrics: the onset of a session is its first event second; an event counts as detected if an alarm is raised at or after onset; the detection delay is the time from onset to that alarm; and alarm fractions are computed before onset and in sessions without any event.

\emph{Statistical inference.} Analyses that need one prediction per sample use the seed ensemble (Section~\ref{sec:seeds}). Ninety-five percent confidence intervals are percentile intervals from a bootstrap that resamples the \nGroupsEval{} linkage groups (2,000 replicates). Paired comparisons use the same test samples. The primary paired test is a group bootstrap of the cost difference; its two-sided $p$-value is $2\min\{\Pr(\Delta^*\le0),\Pr(\Delta^*\ge0)\}$ over the replicates $\Delta^*$, so with 2,000 replicates it is resolved only to about 0.001. The \nSamplesEval{} test seconds come from only \nSessionsEval{} sessions, \nGroupsEval{} groups and \nEventSessions{} sessions with an onset, which is the relevant sample size for inference. With so few groups of unequal size, cluster-bootstrap inference is approximate and can be anti-conservative~\cite{cameron2008bootstrap}; we therefore read $p$-values between 0.01 and 0.05 as approximate and also report an exact sign-flip test (analysis (v)). The intervals describe the sampling of test groups given the trained models; they do not include retraining variability beyond the five seeds. McNemar's test~\cite{mcnemar1947,dietterich1998tests} (exact binomial and continuity-corrected $\chi^2$) is also reported, and its contingency table is checked against the error counts ($b-c=\mathrm{err}_B-\mathrm{err}_A$); because consecutive seconds of a session are strongly dependent, this sample-level test overstates the evidence and is secondary.

\emph{Post-hoc analyses.} (i)~Holm's step-down adjustment~\cite{holm1979simple} of the six group-bootstrap $p$-values comparing E-DDQN with the other primary models, per operating rule. (ii)~A paired group bootstrap of each model's cost against the always-alarm policy, Holm-adjusted over models. (iii)~Two grouping sensitivity analyses: a bootstrap that resamples the \nSessionsEval{} sessions instead of the \nGroupsEval{} groups, and a leave-one-group-out jackknife of every cost and cost difference. (iv)~Validation-chosen cost curves: for each false-negative to false-positive cost ratio $R\in\{1,1.5,2,3,4,5,7,10,15,20\}$, the threshold minimizing $R\,\mathrm{FN}+\mathrm{FP}$ on the pooled inner-OOF scores of each outer fold is applied to that fold's test data, and the test cost is divided by the cost of the better trivial policy at the same $R$ (values below 1 mean that the model beats both always and never alarming). (v)~From the saved predictions only: ROC-AUC computed within each outer fold and averaged over folds, next to the pooled ROC-AUC, which combines the scores of five fold-specific models; an exact two-sided sign-flip permutation test of each paired cost difference with the \nGroupsEval{} groups as units (all $2^{21}$ sign patterns), Holm-adjusted like (i) and (ii); and an exploratory comparison of the within-fold ROC-AUC of selected configurations with and without the questionnaire indicators.

\subsection{Step 8: Telemetry-Perturbation Stress Tests}
\label{sec:threat}
\emph{Threat model and scope.} The motivating adversary controls the rendering or locomotion pipeline (e.g., through a malicious modification or a compromised runtime) and can degrade frame pacing (Type~I)~\cite{odeleye2021framerate}, distort camera or locomotion motion (Type~II) or manipulate the field of view (Type~III) to induce or exploit cybersickness~\cite{tseng2022dark,kundu2025securing}. The dataset contains \emph{no attacked sessions} and no measurement of what a manipulation would do to a participant. We therefore use perturbations only as a sanity check of which inputs the detectors rely on: synthetic, bounded changes of the logged telemetry of the outer-test sessions, after which the causal features are recomputed with the frozen, training-fitted featurizer and scaler, as a deployed monitor would see them. Labels are never changed.

\emph{Variants} (fixed before evaluation; values in Supplementary Sec.~S4.1). Frame-rate capping (Type~I), orientation jitter, speed up-scaling and acceleration-spike injection (Type~II), each at three strengths and applied to the whole session or to one random 60-s window, give 24 variants, all clipped to the ranges observed in the data. Field-of-view manipulation (Type~III) is not evaluated, because the field of view never varies within a game in the data. Five further whole-session ``hiding'' manipulations (speed down-scaling, orientation smoothing and frame-rate restoration) are available only to an adaptive adversary. For each model and test session, this adversary picks among the 17 whole-session variants (12 stress scenarios and 5 hiding manipulations) the one that minimizes alarms on event seconds (\emph{evasion}; the labels are known to it) or the one that maximizes alarms on non-event seconds (\emph{false-alarm induction}). Choosing among fixed candidates gives lower bounds on vulnerability that are weaker than gradient-based attacks~\cite{kundu2025securing,ahmed2025adversarialvr}; the tests are feature-space stress tests, not demonstrations of physical rendering attacks.

\subsection{Step 9: Explainability}
\label{sec:shap}
We explain the decision margin $\Delta Q(s)=Q(s,1)-Q(s,0)$ of E-DDQN rather than a single Q-value, because $Q(s,1)$ alone also contains the state value $V(s)$, which is shared by both actions and does not explain the decision. SHAP values~\cite{lundberg2017shap} are computed for one model per outer fold, retrained with the configuration, seed and inner split of that fold's first final model, with 200 background samples drawn from its training rows and attributions for 400 of its test samples. DeepExplainer~\cite{shrikumar2017deeplift} was tried first with its additivity check enabled; in every fold it failed that check (it does not recognize the layer-normalization modules of the selected networks), so the expected-gradients estimator of the SHAP library (GradientExplainer) was used instead. The method used in each fold is recorded with the released results.
